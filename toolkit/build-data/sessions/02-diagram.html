Latour's ideas

  • Critique of modernism. Modern thinking splits the world in two: human or thing, mind or matter. Latour rejects this dualism and proposes symmetry: humans and non-humans are studied in the same way. The professor added a warning: in theory they are symmetrical, but in everyday life a person can still refuse the key, so they are not fully equal.
  • A construction. ANT is not only a point of view; it works in steps. It separates actors from actants, it is translational (cultural and political values are translated into objects), and it is always multi-network: there is never only one network.
  • Black box. We can see what goes into the system and what comes out of it, but we never fully know what happens inside.
  • Human + non-human. The non-human side can be material, like a key, or immaterial, like a rule or a habit.

How an object becomes an actor (read from bottom to top)

  1. Interest (the input): a cultural or political wish, such as safety or identity.
  2. Translated: design and technology turn the interest into something we can make.
  3. Actant: the object exists, but it is not yet active. Not every key is a Berlin key.
  4. Performs: through use, function and users the object starts to act. This can go back and forth; an active object can become passive again.
  5. Actor: now the object really changes what people do.
  6. Re-enforcement, or inscription (the output): the object writes the interest into culture and everyday life.

The Berlin key, step by step

Safety and privacy (interest) → locking (translation) → a key (actant) → daily usage (performance) → the Berlin key (actor) → it shapes Berlin housing and social life (inscription). The same interest could also be translated in another way, for example into lighting and a streetlight. This is why one interest can grow many networks.

In one sentence: an object becomes an actor only when an interest is translated into it and people really use it; then it inscribes that interest into everyday life.
