Pandaemonium: Daemons for Weimar. Introduction Project DTAD, Winter Semester 2026/2027. Studio Brief. Informatics in Architecture and Urbanism (InfAU), Bauhaus-Universität Weimar.

Slide

Concept. A daemon is a program that runs in the background. It has no user interface, waits for an event or a moment in time, wakes up, does its work and falls silent again. The term was coined at MIT in the 1960s. It goes back to the Greek daímōn: a spirit, an intermediary between gods and humans, a power of fate, and the warning inner voice that Socrates called his daimónion. In animist traditions such spirits live in animals, trees, rivers and things, and give people support, orientation and fear in equal measure.

In this studio we install daemons in the city of Weimar. Every project designs a space and a daemon that inhabits it: an artificial intelligence that listens, remembers, warns, negotiates or acts, and that changes the space it lives in. Together, the daemons of the studio form a small Pandaemonium spread across the city. The daemons are developed with agentic AI coding ("vibe coding") and the latest AI models, and are presented as working prototypes in an exhibition at the end of the semester.

1. Starting Point: The Daemon. The studio brings together three meanings of the word daemon:

  • The daimon of antiquity. In Greek religion and philosophy, the daímōn is an intermediary between gods and humans, neither good nor evil but ambivalent. It appears as a power of fate and as a warning voice of conscience. Socrates' daimónion never told him what to do; it only said "no." The Romans knew the genius loci, the spirit of a place, and kept the Lares, the guardian spirits of the household, in small shrines in their houses. Goethe, who lived and worked in Weimar, wrote about the daemonic in Dichtung und Wahrheit and opened his poem Urworte. Orphisch (1817) with the stanza Daimon.
  • The technical daemon. A background process in Unix and Linux systems (sshd, crond, httpd). It has no controlling terminal, listens for events, wakes on a signal or at a scheduled time, writes a log, and can be reloaded, paused or terminated. It is noticed mainly when it acts or when it fails.
  • The animist daemon. Animism sees spirits in animals, plants, rivers and things, each with its own powers. Such beliefs can be understood as a lost psychological support: a way of living with forces we do not control, of being accompanied, warned and consoled. Each of us has known it once. Small children treat their toys as living things, and most of us lose that feeling as we grow up.

Today, AI models can listen, see, speak, remember, reason and act through tools. Intelligence has become a utility. Like electricity, it is available on demand. A sensor, a small computer and a network connection are enough to give a space or an object access to it. For the first time, it is technically possible to build something that behaves like a daemon in all three senses. This studio asks what architecture can do with this intelligence, and what it means for the city.

2. Task Concept. Students work in groups of up to three; larger groups are not allowed. Each group designs a daemon for a specific place in Weimar, consisting of:

  • a space: the daemon's dwelling, an architectural intervention in the city;
  • a daemon: an AI-based agent that inhabits this space, perceives it, and changes it;
  • a working prototype for the final exhibition: a 1:1 spatial fragment or a working model (1:10 to 1:20) with the daemon running live.

The daemon can be invisible and known only by its effects, or it can have a visible, aesthetic presence. Either way, it must be understood spatially: as something that lives in architecture and acts on architecture.

A daemon is not a script. We are not looking for rules like if someone does this, that happens. A rule-based system behaves in foreseeable ways. A daemon gets senses, a memory, a character, goals and ways to act, and works out for itself what to do. Its behaviour should emerge from the encounter between the model, the space and the people in it, and it should be able to surprise its own designers. Students design the conditions for this behaviour: what the daemon perceives, what it wants, what it remembers, what it can change, and what it must never do. Because the behaviour is not fixed in advance, it has to be observed.

The studio explicitly looks for experimental uses of current AI technology in urban space. Conventional media architecture (façade projections, smart shading, responsive lighting, screens, dashboards) is not the aim of this studio (see section 7).

Slide

3. Space and Daemon. Spirits have always had architecture: temples, oracle chambers, grottos, crypts, reliquaries, household shrines, thresholds and wayside shrines. The studio takes this literally. Every daemon needs a dwelling, and every dwelling is designed.

  • The space is the daemon's body. Its openings, thresholds, acoustics, materials, light and paths determine how the daemon perceives the world and how it expresses itself.
  • The daemon changes the space. It can open and close, resonate or fall silent, admit or refuse, rearrange, accumulate or erase. It changes the conditions and rules of a space, not a picture on its surface.

Possible spatial types include the chamber, niche, booth, threshold, passage, crypt, shrine, pavilion, tower, cave, bridge and courtyard: the smallest and most archaic rooms of the city.

4. Site. Weimar is a small city with an extraordinary density of history: Classicism (Goethe, Schiller, Herder, Wieland), the Bauhaus, the Weimar Republic, National Socialism and Buchenwald, the GDR, and the present-day city of tourism, students and residents. Much of the old town is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The city already has many spirits.

Possible sites and spatial types include, among others:

  • bridges, gateways, backyards, remains of the city wall
  • the Bauhaus-Universität main building
  • Park an der Ilm: the river, the Parkhöhle, the Tempelherrenhaus ruin, the Römisches Haus, the Borkenhäuschen, Goethe's Gartenhaus

  • Markt, Theaterplatz, Herderplatz, Frauenplan
  • Fürstengruft and Jakobsfriedhof

The groups choose and research their site after the kick-off phase, between 29.10. and Plenum 1 on 12.11.

5. Method. Agentic AI coding (vibe coding) is the central method of the studio. Students develop their daemons in dialogue with AI coding agents. No prior programming experience is required. The studio is accompanied by the seminar AI in Design. It opens with about four inputs on agentic coding:

  • working with coding agents: describing, testing, iterating and debugging;
  • AI models as components of a daemon: language, vision, speech, memory, tool use.

The first input sets up everything needed for coding with AI agents, and every student receives a small starter project. After the inputs, the seminar turns into the AI Lab: the teaching staff are in the room to discuss, build and experiment with the groups.

The studio also discusses the limits of the method: what the agent writes has to be understood, tested and taken responsibility for by the student.

6. Nonsense Project and Sentient Things. The first two weeks are individual and run two tasks in parallel. Both start at the project introduction on 15.10. and are presented on 29.10. The handout has the details.

  • Nonsense Project. Each student draws one theme and one constraint and uses a coding agent to build a quick, deliberately absurd prototype that shows what the daemon would do. Real sensors and AI services are not needed yet. Simulated or invented outputs are fine. The point is speed and courage, not polish: to learn the tools by using them and to find out what an AI-driven thing in the city could be.
  • One sentient thing. Each student researches one object or place that people treat as alive, aware or able to answer, such as the Magic 8 Ball, the Tamagotchi or an oracle. How does it work, what makes it seem alive, and how do people live with it?

Groups form after the presentations on 29.10. Each group then chooses a place in Weimar (see section 4).

Slide

Constraints.

  • 1. Your daemon only says no.
  • 2. Your daemon has one sense, and it isn't sight. It hears, smells, or feels touch, vibration or temperature.
  • 3. Your daemon is awake for one minute every hour and sleeps the rest.
  • 4. Your daemon remembers only its last visitor.
  • 5. Your daemon cannot speak or write. It acts only by changing the space: opening, closing, moving.
  • 6. Your daemon is a crowd: several small daemons arguing with each other, and the loudest wins.
  • 7. Your daemon works for its building, and the building wants something its users don't.
  • 8. Your daemon's only instructions are a text written before 1900. You choose it and may not add a word.
  • 9. Your daemon lives on offerings. Without them it falls asleep.
  • 10. Your daemon is invisible, yet everyone knows it is there.
  • 11. Your daemon is mortal. It ages and one day stops. People must be able to tell how much time it has left.
  • 12. Your daemon misunderstands the world, and the misunderstanding must be useful.
  • 13. Every thought costs your daemon something visible. A candle burns down, water drains away, a pile of sand shrinks.
  • 14. Your daemon has a rival elsewhere in the city, and they want opposite things.
  • 15. Your daemon is slow. Nothing it does takes under a year.

8. Ethics, Privacy and Permits. Daemons are ambivalent. Every project has to address what its daemon may and may not do.

  • Privacy: consider GDPR.
  • Heritage: interventions at listed buildings and in the World Heritage area must be temporary, reversible and coordinated in advance.
  • Costs: each group works with a limited budget for AI services. Hardware and material are partially provided.
